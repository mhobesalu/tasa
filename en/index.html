A study planner for children

Only today. One step at a time.

Sometimes there are so many school tasks that a child finds it hard to know where to start or how to plan. TASA takes tasks and deadlines from the school e-diary, splits the work into steps of about 25 minutes, and shows the child only today.

Why

Seeing a whole month of deadlines and tasks is too much

The e-diary shows everything: tests, essays, books to read and tomorrow's homework. For many children, that is overwhelming. They don't know where to start or how to plan, so they never start.

TASA doesn't replace eKool or Stuudium, Estonia's school e-diaries. It takes the information that is already there and turns it into one clear next step for the child.

How it works

From the e-diary to today's plan

  1. Tasks come from the e-diary

    Homework, deadlines, tests and grades come from eKool or Stuudium through the official interface, with the school's permission. Teachers don't need to do anything extra.

  2. TASA splits tasks into small steps

    Big work is split into steps of about 25 minutes, taking deadlines and the amount of work into account.

  3. Every tick is a celebration

    The child sees one “Do this now” card. Each finished step, or tick, brings an encouraging line to the screen. When the whole day's tasks are done, there is a small celebration on screen.

What TASA does

A plan that helps the child think ahead

TASA's long-term goal is to teach children self-management and time management, so that one day they can split their work into steps and plan their time on their own.

Test preparation

Revision starts 7 days before a test, especially in maths, chemistry and physics.

Retake plans

A grade below 3 (the default, which can be changed), or “not passed”, automatically becomes a retake plan. A parent can upload a photo of the marked test, and TASA plans the retake revision so the child can practise the problems that went wrong. TASA never solves or explains problems for the child.

Books into pages

Enter the book, the current page and the deadline. The plan says exactly “read pages 41–60”.

Leftovers come first

Whatever wasn't done yesterday is at the top of today's list. Nothing gets lost.

Required events

A weekly maths consultation, for example, shows up marked “Required”.

Parent view

A parent can excuse a task when the child is ill and mark rewards as paid. The child doesn't see the parent view.

Rewards

Effort is visible. The parent decides what it means.

One reward that the parent defines: its name, its unit, and how much each step or full day earns. The child sees the balance.

The evidence

Teaching planning is one of the best-proven school interventions for ADHD

Organisation and planning skills training

A review of school interventions for ADHD found that teaching organisation and planning skills is among the best-supported approaches.

Evans, Owens, Wymbs & Ray (2018), Journal of Clinical Child & Adolescent Psychology

HOPS: homework planning at school

280 middle-school students with ADHD. A short school programme (16 sessions of 20 minutes or less) reduced homework problems and improved organisation and planning, as rated by parents and teachers.

Langberg et al., Journal of Consulting and Clinical Psychology · VCU news, 2017

TASA makes those steps part of the child's every day: the goal is clear, a timer runs, and every finished step is celebrated.

Privacy

A child's data belongs to the child

The parent holds the account

In Estonia a child under 13 can't give consent alone, so the parent gives it.

No passwords

Only official access to the e-diary, and for eKool only with the school's permission.

The minimum

No diagnoses or health data are stored.

Data stays in the EU

Hosting and AI services in the EU, with data processing agreements and no training on the data.

Export and delete

Parents can download the data or delete everything with one button.

Oversight

A plain-language privacy notice and a breach plan. The regulator is the Estonian Data Protection Inspectorate.

The story

It started with one family

Maria Hõbesalu built the first version for her son, to help him plan his studying and break it into small bites. It is in daily use. TASA grew from the wish to help other children learn to plan their time too.
